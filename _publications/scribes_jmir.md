abstract: |
  Background: The administrative burden of manual documentation has been a primary driver of physician burnout and a barrier to patient-centered care. Ambient scribes have emerged as a promising solution that uses passive audio capture and natural language processing to automate clinical note-taking. While their impact on clinical efficiency is frequently studied, there is a lack of research on how these tools have impacted interpersonal communication during clinical encounters.

  Objective: This study investigates the communication pathways and interactional shifts that occur when ambient scribes are integrated into primary care, with a focus on how physicians adapt their behavior to accommodate an ambient digital listener.

  Methods: We conducted a qualitative study with 17 licensed family practice physicians in the Greater Toronto Area who use ambient scribes in their daily practice. Data collection included observations from in-person clinical shadowing (n=5) and think-aloud walk-throughs (n=12), each followed by semistructured interviews; reported findings are predominantly drawn from interview data. We analyzed the combined dataset using iterative thematic analysis and open coding to identify affordances and constraints along 3 communication pathways: physician-to-patient, physician-to-patient-and-scribe, and physician-to-note.

  Results: Along the physician-to-patient pathway, participants reported that ambient scribes increased their capacity to demonstrate active listening, with some reporting greater attentiveness to nonverbal cues, yet introducing the technology to patients to obtain consent consumed limited consultation time, particularly when addressing sensitive health concerns. Along the physician-to-patient-and-scribe pathway, participants appreciated how ambient scribes acted as a forcing function for physicians to verbalize their clinical reasoning to enhance patient comprehension, as well as their scribes’ ability to follow and organize nonlinear conversations. However, they reported that scribes frequently omitted implicit information (eg, deictic gestures) or created unintended documentation artifacts (eg, casual remarks about family members) that required post hoc corrections. Along the physician-to-note pathway, participants reported that scribes optimized their note-taking workflows and enabled note manipulation via spoken words for some physicians but recalling patient information to fix transcription errors and support patient follow-ups proved challenging.

  Conclusions: Integrating ambient scribes introduces a triadic interactional dynamic into traditionally dyadic patient consultations. Clinicians must continuously navigate the boundaries between patient-facing empathy and scribe-facing precision, deciding when to articulate clinical reasoning aloud and when to exercise verbal discretion regarding sensitive disclosures. While ambient scribes effectively reduce in-encounter computer screen time, they do not eliminate documentation overhead—they redistribute it from real-time typing to postencounter cognitive recall and verbal note curation. To prevent delayed cognitive fatigue from postencounter error verification, we suggest that future ambient scribes should move beyond passive transcription to incorporate interactive recall scaffolding and context-aware error prevention.
